Breaking edges and hollowing bodies

Chamfer and fillet on chosen edges, walls of equal thickness for boxes, cups and cases.

On this page

  • Offsetting bodies

Chamfering and filleting edges

Sharp edges rarely look good on a printed part, and they are not particularly strong either. A chamfer cuts the edge off at an angle, a fillet rounds it. Both act on the edges you click.

  1. Select the body (or the group).
  2. Click “Fillet” or “Chamfer” in the ribbon, press B for the fillet or Shift+B for the chamfer, or right-click the body and pick it from the menu.
  3. The body's edges light up. Click those that should be affected. Another click takes an edge out again. With Shift you add or remove a single edge.
  4. Set the size and confirm with “Apply” or Enter. Afterwards the window stays open on the new body with the same size, so the next fillet or chamfer can follow at once; “Done”, Esc or the cross closes it. With Shift or Ctrl a click on “Top edges”, “Bottom edges”, “Vertical edges” or “Horizontal edges” adds those edges to the ones picked instead of replacing them.

The panel sits at the top right. If it covers edges you want to click, drag it away by its title bar; it opens there next time too, and so do “Hollow” and “Pattern”. Double-click the title bar to put it back.

Picking a shape from the shape list while the panel is open closes it without applying anything, and the edges you clicked are forgotten. The click then places the new shape instead of picking edges.

In the panel you find:

  • “All sharp edges” selects every edge at once, “Clear” empties the selection.
  • “Radius” for the fillet. For the chamfer you set “Distance” or “Angle”.
  • “Variable radius” (fillet only) lets the radius change along one edge: small at one end, larger at the other, evenly in between, as where a straight wall meets a slanted one. Click a single edge, then set “Radius at the start” and “Radius at the end”. Which end counts as the start follows from how the edge was built; if the larger radius comes out at the wrong end, “Swap ends” turns it round. It works on one edge at a time, so round several edges one after the other. “Edit” brings both values back.
  • “Sharp-edge threshold” decides which edges count as sharp at all. A value of 25° treats only clear kinks as edges and leaves flat transitions alone.
  • “Vertical edges” picks the edges that stand straight up, such as the corners of a box: one fillet then rounds all the vertical corners at once.
  • “Top edges” picks only the edges along the very top of the body. It is the quick way for a cookie cutter: build the outline in a sketch as a stroke, then chamfer the top edges. “Angle” sets how steep the chamfer is; it cannot reach further than the wall is thick.
  • “Bottom edges” picks only the edges along the very bottom of the body, the mirror of “Top edges”. It is the quick way to round or chamfer only the foot of a part.
  • “Chamfillet”, chosen at the top of the chamfer window next to “Chamfer”, makes the printable foot of a part: a chamfer at the plate that blends tangentially into a fillet toward the wall, so the first layers need no support and the edge still ends soft. “Distance” is the height the foot takes up, “Angle” (15° to 75°) the steepness of the chamfer at the plate. Pick the edges with “Bottom edges”.
  • “Only visible edges” hides the edges that lie behind the body from where you look. By default every edge shows through the body, which gets confusing on a busy part; with the switch on you see, and can click, only the edges facing you. Turn the view to reach the others. An edge drawn in red (see below) always shows.
  • “A click also picks edges that continue smoothly”: a click then also picks edges that flow smoothly into each other, for example all edges around a face with rounded corners. The switch only decides what a click picks: the CAD kernel always carries a chamfer or fillet on along such smoothly continuing edges. The panel tells you along how many more edges that happens.
  • “Keep edge size when resizing” keeps the chamfer or fillet as large as it is, even if you scale the body later.
  • “Preview quality” (“Draft”, “Standard” or “Fine”) decides how finely the preview is calculated. For difficult parts “Draft” is faster.

While you adjust the size, PrintCaddy recalculates the preview. It can take a moment. You may keep adjusting; the last value you chose is what gets calculated.

If the size is too large for the edge, PrintCaddy says so and suggests a smaller value. Often one or two millimetres less will do. At sharp angles a fillet needs more room than its radius suggests. On a small screen the panel scrolls down by itself so the message is in view.

When a selection that worked fails after you click one more edge, that edge turns red and is drawn wider: it is the one that cannot be treated together with the others at this size. Click it again to take it out, or make the size smaller. If you click more edges while the message stands, the first one stays red. When there is no earlier selection that worked - the very first click, or a size you just raised - the edges that fail even on their own turn red, or all of them if it is only their combination.

The edge under the pointer is drawn wider too, so you can see which one a click will pick.

Taking an edge treatment back

A treated body can always be treated again. Select it again and open the tool - Fillet, Chamfer or Hollow, each shows the list: under “Undo an edge feature” you see what is already on it. “Undo” takes back a single treatment. Note: if newer treatments lie on top, they go with it, and the panel tells you how many.

Next to a chamfer, a fillet or a hollowing there is also “Edit”. It takes the treatment back the same way, but opens the tool again at once with the same edges and the old value. You change the value and apply it again: a 3 mm fillet becomes a 5 mm one without clicking the edges once more. If newer treatments lie on top, they go with it here too. This holds for edge treatments you make from now on; older ones only offer taking back, and so do single parts of a group.

On a group, say a cylinder with a hole, the treated edge belongs to the finished body, not to one of its parts. So the group becomes a single body. “Ungroup” still works on it: it takes the edge features back first and then dissolves the group, in one step that Ctrl+Z undoes. Take the treatment back as described above and the group returns with all its parts. The easiest way is to treat edges last. That goes for threads too: the edge tool takes a thread as its exact body, so a block with a tapped hole in it gets its edges rounded with the thread in place. Working out a thread's edges takes longer than for a plain shape, a few seconds for an M6 and about half a minute for a G1/2 pipe thread.

Good to know: PrintCaddy works with real CAD geometry for fillets and chamfers, not just a triangle mesh. That is why a rounded edge is still a rounded edge in the STEP export.

A part imported as STEP keeps its CAD geometry too: chamfers, fillets and hollowing work on the body from the file, however finely the part is drawn on screen. STL, OBJ and 3MF bring only triangles, and a finely drawn part can be too dense for the edge tool. If a part is also available as STEP, import that.

Hollowing bodies

Boxes, cups, cases and covers have one thing in common: they are empty inside, with walls of equal thickness. That is exactly what “Hollow” does.

  1. Select the body and click “Hollow”, or right-click the body and pick it from the menu.
  2. Set the “Wall thickness”.
  3. Under “Open sides”, switch on the sides that stay open: “Top”, “Bottom”, “Front”, “Back”, “Left” or “Right”, as many as you like - “Front” alone makes a slot for a drawer, “Left” and “Right” a tunnel. With no side switched on the body stays closed all round, with a cavity in the middle. “Closed (cavity inside)” switches every side off in one click. “Top” is already on when the panel opens; a click on it closes the top again. A body closed all round looks unchanged from outside: the section view shows the cavity.
  4. Decide whether the “Inner edges” should be “Rounded” or “Sharp”.
  5. Click “Hollow”.

The walls grow inward. The outside stays as it is.

The “Inner edges” only matter where the inner walls move apart - at a step in the body or around an opening. The corners of the cavity of a plain box are always sharp, with both settings. For a rounded inside, round the outside edges of the body first (see above), then hollow it: the inside follows with the radius minus the wall thickness. If the rounded inside cannot be built, which can happen with an outline made of very many short straight pieces such as a traced drawing, PrintCaddy builds the same hollow body with sharp inner corners instead.

For every open side, the body needs a flat face at its very edge on that side. Spheres and free-form shapes therefore cannot be hollowed this way. If the wall is too thick for the body, PrintCaddy tells you and asks for a thinner one. A rounded box whose top and bottom edges are rounded as well is such a case: its wall has to be clearly thinner than that rounding, or set the rounding to 0 first.

A body with a pocket - a block with a hole that does not go all the way through, made by grouping a box with a raised hole - can be hollowed too, with the top open: the walls round the pocket are hollowed as well, so a ring of cavity stays between the outer wall and the pocket, open at the top, and a thin cavity lies under the pocket's floor.

Bodies that end in a point, such as a cone or a revolved triangle, have no flat face on top that could stay open. A fully closed cavity does not work there either, because a point cannot be offset inwards: even with a 1 mm wall you get the message that the walls cannot be this thick. Draw the wall into the profile before revolving instead: duplicate the outline, scale the copy down evenly, put it in the middle of the large outline and cut the large one with it, then revolve the whole (chapter Sketches, section Revolve). The distance between the two outlines is the wall thickness.

If you resize a hollowed body later, PrintCaddy hollows it again at the new size right away. The wall stays as thick as you chose, round bodies included. A cylinder you pull further one way than the other becomes an ellipse. Fillets and chamfers you laid on it after the hollowing go on again at the same edges.

Tip: With a 0.4 mm nozzle, 1.2 to 2 mm of wall is a good start: thin enough to save material, thick enough for a sturdy part.

Offsetting bodies

Every face of a body can be moved by the same distance, out or in. That is what a press fit, a lid or a snap joint needs: a gap of 0.1 to 0.3 mm all round while the features stay where they are, which scaling cannot give you.

  1. Select one body and click “Offset”, or right-click the body and choose it there.
  2. Choose “Grow” or “Shrink” and set the “Distance”.
  3. The “Outer corners” decide the outer corners: rounded keeps the offset surface round there, sharp lets the faces run on until they meet.
  4. Tick “Keep the original and add the offset body as a hole” to keep the part as it is and get the grown body as a copy that already is a hole. Group that hole with the mating part and it cuts exactly the clearance you chose.
  5. Click “Offset”.

Without the tick the body itself is offset. It becomes a fixed exact body, like after a chamfer: the list at the top of the panel takes the offset back, and “Edit” opens the tool again with the old distance. Only bodies that are exact CAD bodies can be offset - boxes, cylinders and the other shapes, groups of them, imported STEP parts. A body that is only a triangle mesh, such as many STL imports, often cannot be. A distance that is too big for the body, for example a shrink beyond its thinnest wall, is refused.

Deleting faces

A fillet in the way, a hole in the wrong place, a boss you no longer need: “Delete face” takes it off the body again, without building the body anew.

  1. Select the body and click “Delete face”, or right-click the body and pick it from the menu.
  2. Click every face of what should go: the faces of a rounded edge, the wall of a hole, the side and the top of a boss. A picked face lights up; another click takes it out again. The panel counts the faces.
  3. Click “Delete” or press Delete. Esc closes the tool without changing anything.

The faces around the deleted ones grow until the gap is closed. A box with rounded top edges gets its sharp edges back, a hole is filled, a boss is gone. The body stays exact, so it can be filleted and chamfered again and the STEP export keeps its true faces. Ctrl+Z takes the deletion back in one step. It is also listed under “Undo an edge feature” with the fillets and chamfers, where “Undo” takes it back; there is no “Edit” for it. On a group, the result is a single body, as with an edge treatment.

Pick all the faces of what should go, and only those. If the faces around them cannot close the gap, PrintCaddy says so and nothing changes: a main side of a box, for example, has no neighbour that could take its place. Deleting only the floor of a pocket turns it into a hole straight through, because the walls of the pocket run on to the other side.

“Delete face” needs exact faces: it works on parts imported as STEP and on PrintCaddy's shapes. Whatever PrintCaddy keeps as a mesh has none: an imported STL, OBJ or 3MF, a split, wrapped or simplified body, or a very detailed shape such as a long text. Import a part as STEP if you can. On a detailed imported part, a few faces can be too much for the CAD kernel: after 45 seconds the deletion is stopped and nothing changes. A body resized after an edge treatment while keeping its edge size has no exact faces at its new size until the resize is taken back.
